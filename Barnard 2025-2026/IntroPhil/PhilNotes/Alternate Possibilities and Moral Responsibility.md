By [[Harry G. Frankfurt]]

## Main Text
On the subject of [[Free Will]]
### I
*The principle of Alternate Possibilities* - a person is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise
	Implies that "a person is not morally responsible for what he has done if he did it only because he could not have done otherwise"
	a.k.a. if an action someone performs is solely deterministic, they are not morally responsible for said action
	[[Determinism]] potentially combats this principle - eliminating alternate possibilities - as there is only one line of action that all matter follows
		Remember, [[David Lewis]] says that determinism is compatible with free will (or at least the possibility of choice) as **divergence miracles** can occur 

Treated most often as an *a priori* [[Truth]]

Frankfurt argues that a person who has committed something could not have done otherwise (but can still be held morally responsible for these actions)
### II
Discusses the effect of *coersion* on the subject of "could have done," namely, whether we are released from moral responsibility in events where no choice is present, with an example of three Jones-es. Each Jones was  coerced to commit an act contrary to what he had initially intended to perform, though each differ in how they react to such threats.
==Jones I==: Goes on as if the threat had not been heard at all, continuing to commit his original action. In this case, the threat "had no coercive effect and, hence, it did not actually deprive him of alternatives to doing what he did."
**Jones II**: Concedes to the threat. However, he can only be judged for the decision to concede to the threat, not for his coerced actions afterwards as a result of the coercion.
*Jones III*: Jones' initial action was always aligned with the coercion, and so he was not affected by the coercion whatsoever. His moral responsibility is "unaffected by the threat" and can thus be judged for itself
### III
Either way, whether *Jones III* was coerced or not, his moral responsibility would not have been affected by the coercive act itself
	This is because if *Jones III* is considered to be coerced (even though his action was not a result of the coercion directly) then his action includes his own moral responsibility (at least in some cases)
	Whereas, if he was not coerced, because his action was not a direct result of the coercion directly, then his own moral responsibility is still included in this action, not from the coercion itself
==Check with ding^==

"When we excuse a person who has been coerced, we do not excuse him because he was unable to do otherwise"
"Even though a person is subject to a coercive force that precludes his per- forming any action but one, he may nonetheless bear full moral responsibility for performing that action."

### IV
The case with Jones provides a counterexample to the *principle of alternate possibilities*

***Jones IV***: A case in which a second party, Black, makes sure that, if Jones is about to do what is contrary to what Black desires, coerces Jones to do what Black desires. **However**, Black never has to make a move because Jones was always going to do what he desired. Jones had "no alternative but to do what Black wanted him to do." His moral responsibility for doing it is not affected by the fact that Black was going to change his actions.
	It is a free willed but inevitable action
	Can work for a world based in [[Determinism]] or in[[Determinism]]
### V
In the case where the person could not have done otherwise, even if the coercive threats were removed, then it is silly to think about what he *might have done*, as that's not relevant to the moral responsibility of the individual <u>now</u>

If a person is causally determined to do something, they still are responsible for their actions, even if the notion of could have done is included
	The responsibility is not excluded because of coercion (or causality)

Its wrong to think of freewill as the possibility for alternative possibilities, Frankfurt claims, because of these *Frankfurt Cases*
	It is moreso about the *choice* that the individual took rather than the possibility of alternative worlds
	Almost pulls a [[Maimonides]] (in resolving two seeming conflicting things...) and says determinism and freewill can both be true

### Summary
Dialectically, the argument goes as follows:
1. There exists an intuitive way to think about [[Free Will]]
2. Compare this understanding to the case with ***Jones IV*** and Black - determinism seems irrelevant, as the decision is made by the individual person regardless of the imposing force (as the force doesn't end up showing its hand in influence)
3. There must be a different way to think about free will that doesn't require alternative possibilities

The new story of Free Will that Frankfurt proposes is the course of actions you executed (and decided to do) that influence your current decision
## Thought Cabinet
This argument seems plausible, though fails to accurately describe how Jones II is ignored by the principle of alternate possibilities
In other words, Frankfurt argues that outside circumstances have no outward bearing over what we consider to be moral responsibility

The first three Jones cases where the threat or coercive force is shown, the force's nature is added to the causal history of the individual's actions

The contemporary literature discusses "**Flickers of Freedom**" - a defense for alternative possibilities against Frankfurt
	The thought is: Let Jones IV <u>try</u> to go against Black and fail... there still exists those possibilities... the possibility is in the try
	It's a matter of changing the predictor variable for the future action
	Falls short, as Black could just change the predictor variable (in this arguments case, its most often described as a facial twitch...)

The response of "**Robustness**" - the minor flickers of freedom are extremely trivial and don't truly influence further action in the presence of Black in this scenario
