#philosophy 

A work of [[Existentialism]] by [[Jean-Paul Sartre]]

Addresses the popular understanding of existentialism as "a philosophy for the depressed and the nihilists." [[Jean-Paul Sartre]] attempts to demonstrate that existentialism is inherently a hopeful, human-oriented philosophy, rather than an anti-human, dreadful one.
Sartre goes about this by arguing that existentialism is all about how existence (that is, matter itself, and the world in which we inhabit) precedes essence (similar to the idea of the [[Form]], more accurately in this context as the cultural, social, and purpose-oriented meaning we project onto the self, the body). In this sense, we come to exist and our able to determine the meaning, the essence of our being. I use the pronoun "our" here, because it is not merely the essence of the singular being, but the essence of humanity.

Sartre put it mist clearly when he said "if I decide to marry and have children - granted such a marriage proceeds solely from my own circumstances, my passion, or my desire - I am nonetheless committing not only myself, but all of humanity, to the practice of monogamy. I am therefore responsible for myself and for everyone else, and I am fashioning a certain image of man as I choose him to be, In choosing myself, I choose man"

It is here where I think of [[Kongzi (孔子)]], of how leading a life of effortless action shapes others to do the same, shapes [[Good (and Ren, 仁)]] to come unto you. Though Sartre did not so much make the same value claims that Kongzi did, the notion of this "effortless action (wuwei)" or following the [[Way (Dao, 道)]], shaping the world around you carries over, as it is all of mankind that effortlessly sculpts the idea of what man is, whether intended or not. We are all responsible for the image of ourselves that appears in the reflection of humanity. 
What's more, it is a fragment from Kongzi from the [[Analects]] that reads "Is it not ‘sympathetic understanding’ (shù 恕)? Do not impose upon others what you yourself do not desire." Sartre proposes that every man ought to be asking himself a similar question "Am I really a man who is entitled to act in such a way that the entire human race should be measuring itself by my actions," a message Sartre borrows from [[Søren Kierkegaard]]'s notion of the Leap of Faith and the *Anguish* of Abraham. It is this notion of ourselves being inextricably linked (and determined by) the actions of others around us, while also determining the fate of others. It is the [[Golden Rule (and shù, 恕)]]

Thus, the value of any action we commit (as all actions are steeped in Kierkegaard's anguish, so Sartre says) only comes from how it affects the people around us. This idea is mirrored in the Marxist ([[Karl Marx]]) sense. That is, that surplus value comes not from the inherent value of the commodity, but from the labor power projected onto the product by the laborer. Here, a clear, value-giving relationship through the laborer (which is what we all are, socially) and their action.

So, Sartre's idea of abandonment (merely that god does not exist), anguish (borrowed from Kierkegaard, though applied more broadly to imply a greater sense of freedom; that existence precedes essence), and despair (that all we are are the actions we embody, and nothing more, that we have to act without hope because we can control only what's up to us) combine to create [[Existentialism]]

Replies that existentialism is a philosophy of *quietism* (relinquishing that things are just the way that they are, or that we are helpless to do anything), but asserts the opposite - that it is our responsibility to shape and contribute to the human project

Founded on the Cartesian ([[René Descartes]]) *[[Cogito]]*, that "I think therefor I am." Sartre extends this fact to other people, demonstrating that we must believe this to be true if we are to value the actions of other people, as well as our own actions (every good theory is grounded in a truth). It is subjectivist because we come to understand the meaning of our actions for ourselves; it is intersubjectivist because others do the same to us, and vice versa. 

Well then, where do we get our morals, if not from God? Do we make way for [[Moral Relativity]]? Maybe, though Sartre says that all morals are an act of creation, similar to the creation of art. We aren't able to know what tomorrow's painting will be, and we can't judge the painting as we would the finished version of it. It is an act of invention (in the name of freedom). There are no *a priori* [[Truth]]s here. We are all not in disarray and moral entropy because we exist in relation to one another and must consider our own benefit (and thus the benefit of our surroundings).

By humanism, he does not mean that man is valuable because of all the great feats we (or maybe just a few of us) have accomplished, but rather that the only perspective that exists is the human one, and that the meaning of man is constantly being informed by man's actions (very similar to the Butlerian ([[Judith Butler]]) notion of performativity)
