1. Early period

### Closing Remarks
This text was really a treat to read. Out of Plato's works, I have a strong feeling that this one neither overstays its welcome nor underdelivers in both the complexity of its ideas and style by which it explores them. Plato's exploration of Love was incredibly refreshing, and the diverse perspectives offered by the guests of the Symposium highlighted the exciting parts of the [[Republic]]'s dialectics (namely the exchanges with Glaucon and Thrasymachus).
It is really beautiful too see such creativity and complex thought from two and a half millennia ago. The cultural references, though a bit more formal than what I'm used to, feel like a modern version of an internet forum or white-guy podcast. And yet, the honesty with the homo-erotic relationships is surprising, especially when contrasted with the general narrative offered by "The West" that tends to exclude this homo-eroticism. All of the characters are witty and poignant, even if some only get a few pages of dialogue. Every page feels necessary and full of content. 
The ideas presented, mostly in terms of praising, analyzing, and categorizing Love, all offer what feels like a pretty decisive take on how to interpret the subject. Although, in true Socrates fashion, no answers are given, just the methods by which such truths can be chased.
In terms of any outstanding questions, I am curious about adapting these frameworks to modern structures of erotic love (Nuclear family, t4t, polygamy, sexual commerce), as well as exploring the love present in all desires (which honestly could've been explored more by Socrates' account of Diotima's framework). 
Aristophanes' myth of the lovers has to be, by far, one of my favorite myths, even if it fails to address certain biological and social questions about love (pitfalls inherent to any sort of "soul-mate" narrative.
A truly remarkable text.

### Discussion Post #1
Love is commonly understood as a "force" drawing individuals together. Two lovers are said to be "attracted" to each other, just as opposite charges drift together. In Plato's _The Symposium_, Aristophanes proposes that love (_eros_) acts in this very way, a result of an ancestral separation of conjoined proto-humans by Zeus (190D). To me, the most striking aspect of _eros_ described in _The Symposium_ was not its tendency to draw two individuals together, but its restoration of power through the motivation of fear and shame.

Aristophanes' Myth can be understood through Sigmund Freud's theory of castration anxiety - the fear of losing the _phallus_ (and, with it, the symbolic power we understand it to carry). The conjoined lovers are said to have been more powerful than any individual alone, so much so that they could prove a threat to the gods themselves (190B). While romance and intercourse - a rejoining of the two halves - provide brief reminders of the unity and power once held between two humans, this restoration is forever held in a state of precarity. Scars in the form of the navel act as permanent reminders of our original separation - a castration of power from body. In love, we are given the opportunity to temporarily restore lost power. However, due to our inability to fully restore oneness in love, we become anxious to lose the one who we hold most dear, for fear that we become powerless in our loneliness. 

In the first speech of _The Symposium,_ Phaedrus argues that _eros_ is the greatest of the gods. To do so, he identifies a great power found in the shame that love utilizes: "if a man in love is found doing something shameful... nothing would give him more pain than being seen by the one he loves" (178D-178E). The shame felt when seen by your love, a shame greater than any other, motivates lovers to protect each other. The fear of losing power, stemming from the original castration, acts as a push away from solitude. Shame, on the other hand, prevents lovers from pushing one another away, and restores fractions of the power once found in unity. Love, in this sense, does not function as merely a force of attraction, as opposite charges pulling towards each other. Rather, love becomes a means to reclaim and retain power, however incomplete it may be, propelling individuals away from loneliness through fear and colliding them together through shame.
