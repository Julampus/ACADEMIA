## Chapter 1 - Preface
First-Wave Feminism - A progression of first-wave feminism (which saw the advocacy and earning of equal rights to dress in society, as well as equal rights to suffrage, property ownership, etcetera) and second-wave feminism (a progression in and of itself of the first wave, more so dealing with access to birth control, abortion, and equal pay). Third-wave feminism seeks to undo some of the homogeneity that the second wave evoked with the label “woman,” recognizing as Cohen did, that though identities can be helpful rallying tools, they are also used as a tool for hegemonic oppression. Third wave feminism seeks to recognize the individuality of all women and other gender minorities while uniquely addressing the different problems that their varying identities bring them (e.g. class, race, gender, etc.)

Gender Identity Disorder - The psychological disorder outlined in the American Psychiatric Association’s list of known mental disorders, controversial because, on the one hand it gives trans people a diagnosis to point to to validate their experience (and the insurance companies a disorder to point to in order to provide care for; though it excludes those trans people that simply do not qualify to have this condition), while on the other hand, it inherently implies that trans people are “other” and deviants of the supposed “healthy norm.” This disorder, unfortunately, appears alongside Ray Blanchard’s own taxonomy (i.e.  HSTS, AGP)
## Chapter 2 - One Hundred Years of Transgender History
***1850s-1950s***
Notes from Second Read of the book:
The term transvestite is older (older than transgender; cross dressing ordinances are even older, coming from the 1850’s onward to the 1920s in the United States) - coming from 1910 from Magnus Hirschfield (mostly in association with a sort of erotic desire, though today understood more through the lens of cross-dressing and self expression)

Why was this happening (or at least in the public eye) until now? Likely answer: Cities. People were more exposed to new types of people and were able to explore themselves and express themselves with a greater anonymity and sense of community

The term “transsexual” came later in the 1950s (Though scientists attempted to pathologize gender and sexuality variance since the 1850s), popularized by [[Harry Benjamin]] in order to distinguish those who wanted sex changes and medical procedures from the average transvestite

Magnus Hirschfield - Established the first academic, rigorous study of “transvestites” (which applied to all trans people, at this time), a disciple of Freud and part of his Vienna Psychoanalytic Society, briefly. He established the Institute for Sexual Science, and was one of the first sexologists. Most prominent work came from the 1900-1930. Was targeted by the Nazi’s, with his institute and research being burned down in 1933

Dora Richter - First trans woman to receive sex reassignment surgery (1931); worked with Hirschfield

Karl Bowman - Picked up on sexual research where Hirschfield left off, spiritually at least; studied gay men who were in the army (in an army psychiatric ward for their homosexuality) from the 40s to 50s - was a former president of the APA. The courts cited that sex change operations constituted medical “mayhem”  in 1949 and the doctors who perform them should be repermanded. As such, only a handful of SRS occurred in the 50s, under secrecy. This “mayhem” case connected Bowman to Benjamin, as the two were both on the case (transsexuality at this time was seen as another form of homosexuality). Benjamin objected to the mayhem constitution (that would open up practitioners to persecution) while Bowman supported it. Benjamin was a colleague under Hirschfield earlier in the 20s (before Benjamin’s work on endocrinology truly began) and was likely influenced a great deal by Hirschfield’s studies

Louise Lawrence (trans woman part of the mayhem case) -> Virginia Prince (notable crossdresser and transsexual journal publisher)

Christine Jorgenson - most notable woman to receive SRS surgery, burst into the public eye (formerly in the army, received SRS in Copenhagen and returned to the U.S. a woman; 1952)

Benjamin sought to distinguish Jorgenson from Prince, coining the term transsexual in the 50s.

In the early 50s, Prince was indicted for having relations with another crossdresser over the mail, as well as for publishing a transgender and crossdressing magazine called Transvestia 

Drag balls were occurring prominently from the 60s till the 80s, at the same time as Prince’s clandestine Hose and Heels club

## Chapter 3 - Transgender Liberation
***1950s-1960s***
Trans people of colour (of which haven't been so far as mentioned until now) often faced the brunt of the political persecution thrown at trans people, while the powerful few (namely, white trans people) who were able to organize usually remained isolated from the community at large

See [[Marsha P. Johnson]] - as she was very integral to the formation of transgender liberation movements and stonewall

*1959* - Incident at Cooper's donuts (in LA; Similar to Stonewall, though a whole decade prior). Cops were roaming a neighborhood and doing unwarranted searches on drag queens, trans women, and other queers (often times checking ID; noticing the photo looks different than the queen, and arresting them on the suspicion of vagrancy). At a local coffeehouse, a large number of cops began rounding up all the drag queens. A riot ensued, when queens first started throwing donuts before "fighting in the streets." A majority of people in this group were people of color
*1965* - A similar incident at a restaurant called *Dewey's*, however it was the restaurant owners that were denying service to gender-bending youth for fear that serving them would make the restaurant lose customers. A 150-person sit in crammed into this tiny diner, before they were kicked out (and arrested). A picket followed. (in Philly)
*1966* - After the assassinations of JFK, his brother, and Rev. MLK Jr., the U.S was in turmoil (and the civil rights movement was strained). Another restaurant, (San Francisco) Compton's, called the cops on a table of queens for sitting too long in the restaurant for how much they had paid (apparently a crime). As a cop tried to pull one out, the queen threw hot coffee in the cops face. Another riot ensued. In *Tenderloin* - Made a documentary on this called Screaming Queens

A lot of these riots were contextualized by violence (reminiscent of the BPP's tactics during the civil rights era...)

Due to widespread redlining (especially in Tenderloin, SanFran (where the Compton's riot occurred)), and increased police surveillance during wartime, the conditions in the city made the mistreatment of trans women (especially in prisons or in police custody) easily done without much repercussion (except for social action)

The civil rights movement of the 50s and 60s was inextricably intertwined with the gay rights movement. It was not that one group was borrowing protest tactics from another, but that both contributed to each other for the same goal: Liberation

A youth group called *Vanguard* formed as a result of the Compton incident (of which continued with increased "service charges," security guards, and policy harassment of queer customers) - they rallied together to advocate for and educate one another and make the streets of Tenderloin, San Francisco more of their shared home. They also published a magazine of the same name. This continued resistance by Vanguard and the queens of tenderloin, though unable to change legislation, allowed for an increase in quality of life and the formation of strong communities.

It wasn't until Harry Benjamin released his book in 1966 (about two decades after the mayhem case), *The Transsexual Phenomenon*, that things started to change in terms of whether doctors in the U.S. were willing to provide hormones and surgery for trans patients (without facing persecution for "mayhem"). Benjamin advocated that felt gender could not be changed, and that doctors ought to alleviate the mental dysphoria of patients by offering these procedures. 
This, of course, is an oversimplification of the role gender plays in our lives, and was more so a sweet lie for cissexual people and conservative doctors to take in order to begin to provide care for trans people. -> Led to the formation of many transsexual groups (and police reforms) because of the book's subject's medical, "scientific" basis

One such group was the **National Transsexual Counseling Unit** (**NTCU**) made by FTM philanthropist Reed Erickson (a trans man who inherited a company from his father, ran it successfully, sold it for 40 million dollars, and spent that money on eccentricities such as constructing a residential commune in Mexico; he believed in extrasensory perception and was a nudist) He also established the Erickson Education Foundation, which provided advice for transsexual people seeking social or medical help, and created a foundation that helped fund Benjamin and contributed to the publishing of *The Transsexual Phenomenon*.

**Stonewall** - 1969, drag queens were consistently harassed by police during police roundups at the time (This was occurring in Greenwich Village), riots erupted as police harassment increased; the archetypal homophile rights movement, though of course not the first significant one

Homosexualism was removed from the APA guide to psychological disorders as a part of the homophile rights movement, though transsexualism was soon added. *Gender Identity Disorder* was what it was (and is still) called. This move was spearheaded by none other than Harry Benjamin. Helpful to prescribe people, not so much to make people feel incorporated into society/to provide an accurate example of gender dysphoria. 

## Chapter 4 - The Difficult Decades
***1970s-1980s***
By the *70s*, trans people were being excluded from the broader second wave feminism and progressive movements of the time (being seen as more conservative, somehow, in aligning with the binary) Singers like Beth Elliot were rejected from lesbian solidarity groups, scientists and sound engineers like Sandy Stone were subject to hate speech and harassment. 

Trans men were criticized of leaning into male stereotypes or "giving in" to masculine oppression by radical feminists. One such man, *Lou Sullivan*, was an initial transvestite turned FTM man who was a leader in trans-masc community in the 80s (Founded a group literally called "FTM" in the bay area, now called the Lou Sullivan Society). He had  passed away from AIDs in 1991, the pandemic that contextualized the most fear among trans people in the two decades before the turn of the millennium.

GID, though a disorder, was consistently rejected from insurance companies (argued that it was merely cosmetic)

Janice Raymond wrote "The Transsexual Empire: The Making of the She-Male" - a scathing book conflating transsexualism to Naziism, also saying that "All transsexuals rape women's bodies by reducing the real female form to an artifact, appropriating this body for themselves." Also equated trans women to eunuchs. This truly proliferated the trope of the *transsexual rapist* in the zeitgeist.
## Chapter 5 - The New Wave
***1990's, 2007 (Time of Writing)***
The 1990's, unlike the previous two decades, saw a lot more progress in terms of transgender activism on the legal side of things... with Minneapolis, Minnesota being one of the first to establish civil right protections explicitly for transgender people (among two others...)

Transgender rights movements began cropping up in smaller towns, showing a larger upheaval and push towards trans rights. Surgeries developed, pathologization evolved

The trans movement, like at the start of the difficult decades, was often seen as a movement in the perifery, one that just so happened to align with the ideas that spawned during the end of the Cold War, the peak of the [[HIV (AIDS)]] epidemic, a rise in liberalism, the maturation of the baby boomer generation, etc...

A shift towards the usage of the word "Transgender" as an umbrella term (started by Virginia Prince)

Second wave feminism was often infatuated with the idea of feminist "purity," an idea that strictly excluded people of varying classes, races, and sexualities outside of the white middle to upper middle class lesbian. Thus, a shift began to arise in the 1990's towards a more sex positive climate (that didn't view sex as merely a biproduct of patriarchy) and towards an individuals choice to sexual freedom (that didn't rely on systems of oppression)
	*Thinking Sex* was a piece by Gayle Rubin, Barnard's own, on this very subject
The field of gender studies, too, was birthed from this broader societal shift towards individual freedoms ([[Judith Butler]] came on the scene with [[gender trouble]] and [[Performative Acts and Gender Constitution]])

There was a controversy on whether or not to include the "T" in "LGBT," with many groups assuming transgenderism to merely be a sexual fetish - ironically the same rhetoric that conservatives use to disprove the existence or validity of homosexual people

Intersex activism also began to rise in the 90s, with many non-consensual surgeries being performed on infants (that more often than not sexually isolated them by disrupting their gonadal function).

A distinct, visible queer style also began to explode (though there were, of course, other signalers before), a style that is consistent with what we see today (Docs, piercings, flannels, edge and alt, etc.)

There is a general urge for disclosure of trans people; often times in cases of rape and murder, lawyers tend to cite a sort of "panic defense" due to the utter, sheer, horrific, monstrous shock that a partner could have a different body than what you expect, justifying the murder. This was seen most prominently with Gwen Aruajo in the 90s, though thankfully the murderers were found guilty. If trans people were raped however, they often wouldn't be able to make a case for themselves, as they were often assumed to be prostitutes, deceivers, or "deserving it"

There tends to be such an emphasis on socialization...

Internet + Zines + Avant Garde Art = More cultural impact!
Surveillance state imposed by the 9/11 terrorist attacks have made it now more easily than ever to discriminate against trans people. A bill was passed in 2007 to prevent discrimination based on sexuality and gender, though at the last minute gender was removed from the bill. A gender addendum was added later, though it comes to closely impossible to prove whether discrimination actually occurs unless the person is explicitly bigoted.

The beginnings of statistically and demographical trends were measured for transgender people, showing that they are one of the highest rates of people to be subjected to hate crimes, and have one of the highest rates of suicide, unemployment, unhousing, etc.
