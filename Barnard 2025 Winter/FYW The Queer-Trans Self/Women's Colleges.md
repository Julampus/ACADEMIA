#queer 

Trans people have consistently been excluded (or at the very least treated like an afterthought) in higher academia. Nowhere can this be seen more than in the United State's women's colleges (a number that used to be in the hundreds, now is in the forties since title IX regulations)

It's important to understand the history of Title IX, first and foremost. *Title IX* is a civil rights law that bans discrimination based off of sex or gender. This was especially necessary in a time when universities were limited their student body to only men. Columbia University is an example of such. For the first two centuries of its existence, Columbia only admitted men. It took a group of disgruntled women to form their own (very small) college in a brownstone a couple blocks away for them to receive even a modicum of the education that Columbia men received. 
Barnard College was named after the 10th Columbia president, who had advocated that women get access to earning Columbia degrees. Though for a couple years women were able to study at Columbia, Barnard was eventually constructed, which was where the rest of the women went. 
Many women's colleges didn't have any of the same affiliation to men's colleges (and often struggled t receive support because of it).

Nowadays, most former women's colleges have closed down or become co-ed (e.g Vassar; in the case of Radcliffe, it had merged with its sibling school Harvard). There still appears to be a niche that women's colleges can fulfill, and that is to offer those who are consistently marginalized in education to have an environment that prioritizes them. This is why there isn't as much of a niche to be fulfilled for all-men's colleges (which mostly exist for religious or vocational reasons). TERFs often indicate that women's colleges should only have women, though many trans-men and nonbinary people may not experience the same type of privilege that cissexual man experience in coed schools. It is thus argued that any gender minority who deems it right for them to go to a (predominantly, though not entirely) Women's college should be free to do so.

In 2013, a transgender woman named Calliope was rejected from Smith due to her FAFSA detailing her assigned sex at birth (a requirement, if you haven't made a legal sex change yet). This requires the only trans women to get accepted into women's colleges to be rich (rich enough to have the resources to pass, even; excluding trans women who aren't rich enough or who don't have the time to devote hours of their life to medical care). This sparked pretty widescale protest across college campuses, resulting in changes made to many women's colleges admissions policies to "live and consistently identify as a woman."

That being said, although it is the admissions policies of about half of all women's colleges is open to trans women, there still exist numerous challenges that trans students (and especially trans women face). Accommodations for housing, including bathroom availability and roommate assignments, are often not made for such students (or if they are, they require a constant back and forth for the student to engage in, taking more time than their first year seminars). There exists social isolation due to the presence of unspoken transphobia and TERFdom. There aren't any outlined protections made for students who are experiencing harassment or other gender-related issues. Meanwhile, resources are often taken away from trans students (such as in the case of trans awareness events due to pressures from the federal administration). 

[The Dangerous Experiment](https://www.womenshistory.org/articles/dangerous-experiment): (How Architecture => Control)
Women's colleges were designed to mirror men's colleges, but with the assumption that women would be restricted from most of the freedoms that men were awarded. This was reflected in the architecture of most women's academies at the time, which were often secluded colleges housed entirely in one building so that students could be monitored and held in line.
This was known as the *seminary system*, in which it outlined the students' bedtime, their schedules, when to pray or confess, and when their leisure time would be, all supervised and centralized. 
*Vassar* College (as well as Mount Holyoke) was the principle example of such. When it was constructed, it was - at the time - the largest building in the united states (4-5 stories tall; 1/5th of a mile long). 
Too many friendships were formed in this seminary style setting at Vassar (which of course bred rebellion), and so the founders of *Smith* college decided to split women's dorms and seminar rooms into several "cottages" to emphasize more of a familial dynamic. Women were encouraged to enter town to participate in its library and church (emphasized by the fact that they neglected to build any)
*Radcliffe* college went one step further, not offering any housing (though using the same Harvard professors that taught the men's classes) and instead allowing women to live at home and commute in to receive education. *Barnard* college followed Radcliffe's lead
*Bryn Mawr* followed *Smith's* cottage style, though slowly shattered these senses of control by establishing a Jacobean architectural "quadrangle" - an enclosed quad green area circumscribed by walls of dorms and classrooms.
