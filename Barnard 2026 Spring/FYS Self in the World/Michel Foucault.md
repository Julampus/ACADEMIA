## The History of Sexuality
### We Other Victorians
We live in an age (even today) where sexuality is viewed as repressed. It used to be that we were unrestricted by taboos and prohibitions of sex, sometime around the start of the 17th century. Soon, reforms came that swept sex (non productive and non reproductive) under the rug; reforms that were made by the "Victorian" bourgeoise. The supposed "Other Victorians" would profit off of this sex in the sexual undergrounds of cities. 
By understanding the discourse of who was talking about sex, who wasn't, who would hear about it, and why they would do it, one can construct an understanding of sexuality not as innately repressed, but as deliberately engineered.
1. Is/was sex actually repressed
2. Was it power systems that caused such a repression
3. Is the current movement working in "reclaiming" sex, or is it merely another way to enforce power and narratives about sex
### The Incitement to Discourse
The story, the theory of sexual repression as Foucault puts it, is that sex is repressed (though was once free). Foucault says that it is our fetishistic fascination with such a repressive hypothesis that makes it so that we are obsessed with sex (which, in the modern sense, is an institutionalized form of sex). By refusing to talk about it, or to discuss its "repression," we are contributing to the [[Discourse]] that obscures the magnitude of engineering that sex has undergone

## [[Semiotics]]
Foucault furthered the linguistic and semiotic analysis of [[Ferdinand de Saussure]] and [[Roland Barthes]] by using the broader sociocultural applications of sign analysis to understand power: who could and could not speak - who does and doesn't have the language to make change
Diverted from the tradition of semiotics by emphasizing the historical and discursive (and thus power-based), rather than methodological/scientific aspects, of meaning-making

"Neither the dialectic, as logic of contradictions, nor semiotics, as the
structure of communication, can account for the intrinsic intelligibility of
conflicts. Dialectic' is a way of evading the always open and hazardous
reality of conflict by reducing it to a Hegelian skeleton, and semiology' is
a way of avoiding its violent, bloody and lethal character by reducing it to
the calm Platonic form of language and dialogue."

Understood the institution to act out discourse via. various apparatus (government, law, bureaucracy, technology, discipline, etc.)

Rejected Marxism ([[Karl Marx]]) for its reductionism into classes. Although classes help inform ideology, they do not provide a holistic and dynamic view of how discipline is enacted (namely through knowledge and power, rather than class interests)

Knowledge is always a form of power (Foucault referred to it often as "power/knowledge")
Truth is invented by [[Discourse]]
Power, to Foucault, is something we are all caught up in 
Rather than a linear chain, it can be viewed as a web, where we all instantiate to some degree or another the roles of oppressor and oppressed

Foucault, as with [[Ferdinand de Saussure]], dismissed the traditional view of the [[Subject]] as the author of one's own knowledge, truth, and representations. Rather, we are much more objects to the systems of discourse, language, and power/knowledge.
Yet we can find subjects, not as autonomous, but as subjects of [[Ideology]]: the homosexual, the madman, the criminal - this varies by the discursive system we are discussing
	Only when caught in the discourse-positions does discourse make sense
The subject, through Foucault's theory, is best represented through Velasquez' *Las Meninas*, where the observer, the queen and king, and the princess are all subjects in their own way, and are all observers of the other.
