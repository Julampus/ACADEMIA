In *Against Our Will*, [[Susan Brownmiller]] discusses the hypothetical origins of rape and its relation to the construction of patriarchal society. Brownmiller accuses the mere existence of the penis in biological men as the impetus for rape, saying that as soon as men learned that they could rape, they did rape. This takes a [[Simone De Beauvoir]]ian notion, as women is belittled as object in order to justify rape.
Brownmiller also constructs a history of rape (following judiciary/economic practices regarding rape) as property crime. One can trace rape initially to the ancient Babylonian empire, whereby in Hammurabi's code the man who rapes the woman shall have his wife raped. Later societies would tie a virgin's marriage at 50 silver pieces, and thus the unjust rupturing of a virgin's hymen would be charged at that same amount - the cost of a father's lost marriage. Often times, women were charged just as the man, the reasoning being that if they had just been loud enough, they would've prevented dishonor from their family. Otherwise, the woman could choose (or was more often coerced) to marry her attacker to prevent execution, payment, and dishonor. Gang rapes and marriage kidnappings were all commonplace, as a marriage could not be combatted, even by feudal lords. Oftentimes, however, it was feudal lords who would rape virgins with no repercussions (or the repercussions that would follow would fall on the shoulders of their men). 
Later, under Talmudic and Biblical law, the position of woman shifted from man's property and towards man's desire. One should not covet one's neighbor's wife, nor shall one commit adultery. It is in these moments that it is the adulterer who is at fault, not for infringing the rights to autonomy of women, but of the rights to power, family, status, and pleasure of fellow man. Judicial traditions, their beginnings founded in religious texts, would later place the responsibility of restoring honor of the man upon a woman who has been wronged, as it would be woman who must report the injustices of man. Jurors would remain skeptical to woman fearing a "boy who cried wolf" situation (as reflected in biblical stories of the same nature). This framework of man being punished for infringing on fellow man, not on woman, has carried even to today (until the feminist ideas of woman's autonomy has covered up this notion).
Brownmiller's theories, though provocative and bearing some utility in their description of the evolution of judicial practices in retaliation to rape, fall short in that they do not consider homosexual rape or female-male rape as possibilities (though the former is briefly touched on). What's more, Brownmiller's work necessitates a clear-cut biological distinction between the penis and the vagina, attacker and victim, that is overall reductionist. The notion that first came the phallus and then came patriarchy is flawed. 
Furthermore, this follows the general myth of "oh, men are enacting violence? lets blame the trans women..."
It seems more broadly that there are two prevailing schools of thought on rape:
	The *first* states that rape is an act of sexual violence. This framework is helpful, as one can effectively and objectively deduce an incident of rape by mere observation of causes and effects. The only question remains in the judicial verdict and the severity of the crime. The drawback is that this framework of rape ignores the sexual aspect of the crime, reducing it merely to the violent.
	The **second** states that rape is a violation of consent, which allows the inclusion of BDSM sexualities, though places victims in a state of precarity. The vague boundaries of what consent vs. rough sex is makes this framework inadequate. 
It seems more apt to attribute rape to power, though the issue is the distinction between the performance of violence (e.g. in rape-torture) and the performance of violence in BDSM. This mediates the tension between violence, sexuality, victim, and accuser that the previous frameworks failed to address. How does power inform our understanding of rape?
## MacKinnon
[[Catherine MacKinnon]] points out that rather than asking what makes rape distinguishable from sex, we should ask what makes sex distinguishable from rape. The actuality is that they are near-identical, systemically, in the ways in which they exploit women as sex objects. "All heterosexual sex is rape" - as Andrea Dworkin says
It is more so a crime against monogamy and the property over the female than of a crime against woman's body. This is enforced by the state, which until very recently has declared rape by someone that a woman knows as "less severe," and by a husband, impossible
Rape is injury from a women's point of view under the consent model, it is a crime only when the male does not understand consent under the legal model.
It is assumed that women want sex, it is their desirability after all that is their power over man, so why is it any surprise when a revocation or denial of consent is contested. The male state has deemed rape as a violation of property, not of woman's bodily autonomy. To be treated as object, the inequalities of sexuality, bleed into the living world. It is not that heterosexual sex is inherently bad, it is that the dynamics mirror the world as we know it, with sexuality as its justification. 
In other words, we ought to ask "who has a right to sexuality"
