## Early Philosophy
Wittgenstein argued that [[Language]] limits what kinds of questions we can ask and what kind of meaning we can draw from statements. Just as how the physical world is made up of objects, which then translate to a state of affairs, and finally facts, so too is language made up of names, elementary propositions, and propositions. Language reflects the observable world as Wittgenstein sees it, reflecting meaning and acting as a picture of meaning to the physical world. Linguistic propositions tie together names with logic, which itself limits what can be said about the world. This viewpoint is similar to that of [[Bertrand Russel]]'s in the logicism of mathematics. This is his *picture theory of meaning*
This carries the conclusion that all we can say is of the physical world, and that metaphysic, ethics, religion, and life-problems exist outside such a scope and are linguistically meaningless (yet still important). In a sense this can be seen as protecting the humanities from the grips of science that early Comtean [[Positivism]] attempted to acheive

## Later Philosophy
Still argued that understanding language is the key to solving philosophical problems (and identifying which ones are able to be solved). Differed from his earlier philosophy in the sense that language is not conceptualized as pictorial, but rather as a series of "games."
	This means that we are not just saying/depicting any one thing as with a picture in a picture frame - we play, sing, argue, discuss, joke, express, abstract, etc.
	Meaning is conferred not only by the referent but by the language game that is played
	These games are socially learned
	Knowing how these rules work and following them to a 't' avoids any linguistic misunderstandings - the root of all philosophical problems
