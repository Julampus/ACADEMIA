#philosophy 

An [[Enlightenment]] philosopher who, at the time when atheists cowered behind the term "deist" in fear of being excommunicated from society, brandishes a clear atheistic philosophy. Was born in a Jewish community in Amsterdam, though was rejected by the synagogue for heresy (and the non-belief in god).

Spinoza, in his treaty on theology and politics, as well as his ethics, declares that we ought to conceptualize God as nature, as the culmination of everything in the universe. His physics states that there is one [[Substance (Arche)]] in the universe, and that is the sum of all nature: *deus sive natura*. What we come to understand mind (existence in thought) or matter (existence in space) as is merely a mode or genre which substance exhibits. This connects the [[Mind (Nous)]] to the body in a way which neatly, if though vaguely, solves the mind-body problem. Argued clearly for a separation between faith and reason, stating that the two operate on separate spheres. Individuality occurs because Spinoza states there are an infinite number of modes that substance is able to exhibit, allowing us to differentiate between substance.
Spinoza's concept of [[Freedom]], though counterintuitive, has a beauty in its praise of reason (which [[Immanuel Kant]] would later critique). Freedom, as Spinoza defines it, is the lack of causation from external factors (that is, being a passive object) and a clear utilization of causation from the internal (that is, being an active subject). Using [[Reason]] as a tool to accentuate and define one's own individuality, free from the constraints of others. One can reason, though, that rational thought converges at a point (this, however, places sensation at the bottom of the epistemological hierarchy). As such, pure reason seems to strip people of their own individual "choice," though in Spinoza's deterministic ([[Determinism]]) system, we are distinguishing between shades of freedom. This freedom that Spinoza describes appears to be anarchist and individualistic, a blatant rejection of absolute authority that theorists like [[Thomas Hobbes]] argued for.
