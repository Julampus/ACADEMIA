## Philosophy
The Renaissance saw a great uptick in the value of the liberal arts: grammar, poetry, music, visual arts, sculpture, and rhetoric. A shift from the minute [[scholasticism]] of the Medieval Period meant philosophers could spend time on different issues, namely [[Aesthetics]] and [[Social Philosophy]]. Platonism, a less religious version of [[Neoplatonism]], was introduced as the upheaval of platonic thought and its reconciliation with biblical scripture. The concept of the form of Good was notably interpreted as God.
[[Humanism]] was introduced into the cultural dialogue during the Renaissance, with popular consensus labelling Petrarch as the "father of humanism," namely for his revival and translation of a great deal of ancient texts, including those of [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]], to Latin rather than keeping them in Ancient Greek. Their accessibility also became apparent with the invention of the printing press, increasing the efficiency by which literature could be pumped out, and the larger scale prevalence of academies and universities. One such university was called the Platonic Academy, though "The Academy" from ancient times bears little resemblance aside from namesake. 
Political and Social theory also developed greatly at this time. Especially in the tumultuous city-states of Italy at the heart of the Renaissance, political theory was booming. [[Niccolo Machiavelli]], the advisor to the ruler of Florence during political revolution from the Medici family to a republican country, argued that a ruler, rather than needing to personify the [[Virtue (Arete; De 德)]], which really only affect the individual, a ruler should personify the Lion and the Fox. That is, the ruler should be both fierce and cunning, prioritizing not ones own individual interests (or even concepts of [[justice]]), but rather every decision should be made for the betterment of the state. 
Later, Machiavelli would revise his thoughts, saying that it is *glory* that every state seeks (the best state, he determines, is that of the republic, as it exemplifies the best interests of the people when the ruler may disagree). This is a main precursor of modern nationalism.
[[Thomas More]] contributed his conception of a [[Utopia]], a derivative of Plato's [[Republic]]. More argued that we are not all seeking glory, but tranquility and leisure (so that we may pursue the virtues individually). In order to achieve a society like this, we must abolish all forms of property, wealth, and other distractions to this end. It is difficult to say with certainty whether or not this would work, however.
