#philosophy 

[[Enlightenment]] philosopher, contributed to [[Empiricism]] and Moral [[Subjectivism]]

Famously stated that "one cannot derive an 'ought' from an 'is'" or "one cannot derive a prescription from a description"

Argued that our beliefs of induction, causality, and the existence of an external world cannot be rationally justified, and yet we cannot help but take them to be true because of 'human nature'

Divided knowledge between **impressions** and *ideas*, the latter being a less intense and further abstracted version of the former.
Impressions are supposedly atomized, indivisible, and fundamental properties of experience that we find in our consciousness (e.g. the feelings gained from a painting, the brightness of sunlight) while ideas are our recollections and representations of such. We connect ideas through causal, spatiotemporal, and material relation in order to form new ideas. However, no matter how hard we try, we cannot identify moral imperatives from impressions. For instance, willful murder does not have the indivisible, fundamental, and observable impression of "one ought not do this." Rather, we deduce such a judgement from ==sentiment==, which is what we observe in our consciousness. Subjectivism was also called sentimentalism in this era for this reason
