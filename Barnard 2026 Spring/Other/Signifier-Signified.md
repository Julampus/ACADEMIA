## THOUGHT_CABINET
Criticism comes broadly at the dismissal of the signifier - it functions as referential and thus informs a great deal of the particular instantiations of a sign (e.g. a particular book and its classification as "book").
It is interesting how we differentiate across grammatical meaning in different languages, completely changing how we approach the concept of the verb while still retaining the signified, to some extent (делать vs. сделать)
An action varies just as much as a noun in terms of context - who is doing it, what kind of thing is doing it, when is it happening, for how long is it happening, to whom is it happening, etc. - it seems verbs are just as arbitrary, and so it is less so about the linguistic symbols, the signifiers, that relate them as it is the <u>logical</u>, <u>grammatical</u>, and <u>lexical</u> meanings that we use to psychologically associate them.
	These, too, vary in terms of cultural context, what one is exposed to, how one views the self, how one views possession, love, etc. Pattern too, it seems, follows some sort of arbitrariness.
	There do exist [[linguistic universals]] which appear to be more so archetypal to the human experience than conceptually necessary. This, too, emphasizes the role of human condition, psychology, and conscious experience as influences of the reception, interpretation, and reaction to meaning. And this is only about linguistic meaning! There are other ways signs show up in life (literal signposts, warning signs, hazardous chemicals (itself, species dependent), environmental hazards (this too is contextually dependent))

That being said, though the reception of the signifier in language (speech) is mostly arbitrary, physiology plays a major role in what we can receive and how we can interpret it. ([[Chapter 41 - Sensory Systems]]) Species capable of vision will almost universally view flashing lights as distressing, audition will cause fear in the event of a loud bang, somatosensorial will cause distress when pain is inflicted, vestibular system will cause nausea upon vertigo (of course, the issue of masochism arises in any of these cases, and here is where culture and the arbitrary factor take shape, though the interpretation of these systems, too, are dependent on some archetypal human experience). Perhaps this concept is only limited to linguistics, in which I have little expertise, though I would argue that sensation is a fundamental form of language that needn't be ignored (language requires audition for speech, vision for asl, somatosensorial in the case of brail)
