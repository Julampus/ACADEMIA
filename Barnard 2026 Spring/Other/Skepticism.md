#philosophy 

Figureheaded by the sixth leader of the Academy Arcesilaus, following the [[Plato]]nic tradition of belief as relative and bodily and knowledge as eternal and mental, skepticism is defined by its mistrust in any knowledge. 
Five tropes were proposed that encapsulate the concept of the suspension of judgement that skepticism advocates for:
1. When we discuss knowledge of a subject, there are often two sides with opposing views. The very fact that a subject can be argued about means we should suspend judgement.
2. We often base our arguments in assumptions that themselves need to be argued for. These assumptions are based in other beliefs that need to be argued for. This continues *ad infinitum*, so we ought to suspend judgement.
3. Often times our arguments depend on a conclusion that the arguments argue for (circular reasoning), so we must suspend judgement
4. All sensation is relative and yet forms the basis of belief, so we must suspend belief
5. Some assumptions are merely guesses which can be substituted for others, so we must suspend judgement

This parts from the belief that [[Stoicism]] holds where all actions are based in belief (and what we believe is only expressed by our actions). Beliefs are informed by our perceptions, which are causally determined ([[Determinism]]; e.g. the colour of an apple is determined by the apple, not our intend to observe it). 
Rather, skeptics argue that we must act as though we suspend our beliefs, or at the very least act in such a way where the most plausible belief (firmly explored and verified against other beliefs) must be acted upon. Some skeptics even still go on as though even this inference of best guess functions as a form of knowledge, and goes about the world in a sort of [[Ataraxia]]

Highly influential to later [[Enlightenment]] thinkers
