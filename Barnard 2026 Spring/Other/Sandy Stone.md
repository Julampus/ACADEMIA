## The _Empire_ Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto
In response to [[Janice Raymond]]'s comments of transsexuals "raping all women" by appropriating and sullying their image (including that of feminist movements and lesbianism)

Stone responds to he comments made by Raymond, highlighting how many early autobiographical accounts of transsexualism were exaggerating their claims or even, to some extent, fetishizing womanhood, often employing heterosexism and oppositional sexism to legitimize their instantaneous transitions. Stone astutely points out that a majority of transsexual individuals read [[Harry Benjamin]]'s *The Transsexual Phenomenon*, which proposed a set of hormonal and surgical procedures that soon became near-standardized in gender-affirming clinics (which were prior doing no-questions-asked surgeries, according to Stone). It seems the tracks were laid before the train, in this sense. The category of transsexual was manufactured through the lens of a white, upper-middle class, cissexual male gaze that came to understood the transsexual feminine desire as one for heterosexual womanhood, and furthermore dysphoria (which was implemented in the 90's and still exists today in the DSM-5) as a sense of being "in the wrong body". Transsexual women had no choice but to conform to these notions in order to seek the treatment that they needed (as Stone says, it is the goal of every transsexual to pass). This further informed the notion of Benjamin's transsexual, as now there was more empirical evidence that there is some hegemonic transsexual experience that can be diagnosed and attended to.
This places visibility in a sort of double bind: a trans person must consistently seek passing (for prudential reasons: safety, comfort, pleasure) which inherently involves enmeshing oneself into hegemonic cissexual culture (reducing visibility of transsexual experience) and yet visibility of the diversity of transsexual experience is necessary for the system itself to change.
This appears to be a triple bind, in my opinion, as transgender visibility now leads to it being harder to pass, as now people are equipped with the tools to clock someone much more consistently than they could have been able to before. 
Stone concludes by advocating that we start, first on the individual and then on the community based level, reevaluating how we come to understand transsexualism and our conceptions of the body. Remediating Raymond's scathing critiques, Stone offers that we conceptualize trans women as a *genre* of women, evoking the Lordian ([[Audre Lorde]]) and generally second wave feminist notion of power in difference. Only when our individual selves are celebrated for our diversities can we come to gain a more complex, holistic understanding of identity, body, and self.
	My critiques for future inquiry mostly come at Stone assuming that a transsexual must desire to pass (though even I find myself agreeing with this at times). It seems that this assumption may even be contradictory to her overall point that we should leave these cissexual defined conceptions of transness in order to achieve some greater sense of clarity.
